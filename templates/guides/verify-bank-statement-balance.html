How to verify a bank statement balance after converting PDF to CSV

Converting is the easy part. Proving the CSV is complete before you import it into QuickBooks, Xero or Excel is what saves rework.

The one formula to check

opening balance + total deposits − total withdrawals = closing balance

Take the opening and closing balances printed on the statement, sum the deposit and withdrawal columns of your CSV, and compare. A difference of 0.00 means no row is missing or double-counted (it does not prove every date or description is right, so still spot-check a few rows).

Step by step in Excel or Google Sheets

  1. Put deposits and withdrawals in separate columns as positive numbers.
  2. =SUM(C:C) for deposits and =SUM(D:D) for withdrawals.
  3. Computed closing: =opening + deposits − withdrawals.
  4. Difference: =computed − printed closing, rounded to 2 decimals.
  5. If the statement has a running balance column, add =E1 + C2 − D2 − E2 down the sheet; the first non-zero row is where the problem starts.

Reading the difference

  • Equals one transaction amount → that row is missing (often at a page break).
  • Equals twice an amount → a deposit was read as a withdrawal or vice versa.
  • Equals a balance figure → a "balance brought forward" line was counted as a transaction.

FAQ

What is the bank statement balance check formula?

Opening balance + total deposits (credits) − total withdrawals (debits) should equal the closing balance printed on the statement. If it does not, at least one row is missing, duplicated, or has the wrong sign.

Why doesn't my converted CSV balance?

Common causes: a transaction split across a page break, a duplicated header or carried-forward line counted as a transaction, a credit read as a debit, or pending items that are not on the statement.

How do I find the missing row?

Compare the running balance column row by row: the first row where the previous balance plus the amount does not equal the printed balance is where the error starts. A difference equal to one transaction amount usually means a missing row; twice the amount usually means a wrong sign.
